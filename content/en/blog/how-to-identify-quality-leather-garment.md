A jacket at 200 euros and another at 2000 euros sometimes carry the same **genuine leather** label. That label tells you almost nothing about what you are buying. Here are the criteria that identify a quality leather garment, and the signs that give away second-rate leather.

## In short

1. **Full grain leather** is the only genuinely high-end grade. Split leather and corrected grain sit far below it yet carry the same genuine leather label.
2. The **grain** of good leather is irregular. A pattern repeating identically means it was embossed, so the hide is corrected or synthetic.
3. The **edge** is the most reliable test. Dense, full fibre means solid leather, a pale foam layer or a textile backing means the opposite.
4. **Finishing** says as much as the hide. Even stitching, a lining that is sewn rather than glued, a zip from a recognised maker.
5. A house that masters its material **names it precisely**. Vagueness about the type of leather is itself a sorting criterion.

## The criteria for identifying a quality leather garment

Five criteria let you judge a piece in a few minutes, in store or when a delivery arrives.

| Criterion | Sign of quality leather | What should worry you |
|---|---|---|
| Type of hide | Full grain, type of leather named on the label | Genuine leather alone, no detail |
| Grain | Irregular, pores of varying size | Pattern repeating at fixed intervals |
| Edge | Dense fibre, full thickness, even tone | Pale foam, visible textile backing |
| Suppleness | Supple from the hide itself, marks under pressure | Stiff, or soft on the surface but cracking at the fold |
| Finishing | Even stitching, sewn lining, branded zip | Visible glue, uneven stitches, unbranded zip |

The first move is to look for the **type of leather**, not the material. A house working fine hides names them, because it is a selling point. Silence on this is rarely an oversight.

The second move is tactile. Quality leather warms to the hand within seconds and holds a faint mark when pressed with a fingernail, a mark that then fades. Corrected leather, coated with a thick pigment layer, stays cool and does not move.

## Full grain, corrected grain and split leather

The whole hierarchy of leather comes down to how the hide was split by thickness. It is the most useful distinction to know, and the one labels most often dodge.

**Full grain leather** is the top layer of the hide, kept intact. It is the densest part, where the fibres are tightest. It keeps its natural grain, with the animal's irregularities, and develops a patina over time instead of wearing out. It is the only grade that earns the word quality without qualification.

**Corrected grain**, often sold as top grain, is that same layer sanded to erase defects, then embossed with an artificial grain and coated with pigment. The result is even and uniform, and much more fragile, because sanding cut through the strongest fibres. It is the most common leather in ready-to-wear.

**Split leather** comes from the lower layers of the hide, beneath the grain. It has no natural surface and always receives a coating or a flocked finish. It can legally be called leather, but its durability bears no comparison.

## How to tell real leather from synthetic

Recent synthetics imitate grain well. Three tests still expose them.

The **pattern** first. A hide is unique, its grain never repeats. A synthetic is printed from a die, so the same drawing returns at regular intervals. Comparing two distant areas of the same piece is enough.

The **edge** next, visible on an unfinished border, inside a pocket or under a flap. Leather shows a full, fibrous thickness, often lighter at the core. A synthetic shows a sharp coloured layer sitting on foam or a woven backing.

The **behaviour** last. Leather absorbs the warmth of the hand and creases into fine, soft lines. A synthetic stays at room temperature and folds into sharper, more angular breaks.

## What the finishing reveals

A fine hide badly assembled makes a poor garment. Finishing is a criterion in its own right, and often the fastest to check.

**Stitching** should be even, with a constant stitch density and no loose threads. On quality pieces the topstitching follows the cut line exactly, including at corners, which is where sloppy work shows most.

The **lining** should be sewn, never glued. A glued lining peels away in patches after a few seasons and cannot be repaired. Its presence is itself an indicator, the most carefully made pieces line the sleeves too.

**Zips** are a discreet but reliable marker. Recognised zip makers, YKK first, then Riri or Lampo at the high end, engrave their name on the pull. An unbranded zip on an expensive garment is a saving made in the wrong place.

## Which houses are the reference for leather

The word quality does not cover the same work depending on the territory, and it is worth knowing before comparing brands.

On the **heritage and biker** wardrobe, houses such as Schott NYC, Belstaff or Aero Leather are authoritative. Their craft is about thick hides, tanned to last, assembled to long-standing patterns. This is the territory of the rugged piece that patinates over decades.

On **couture leather**, the logic is different. It is no longer about resistance but about cut, fit and hand finishing. **Jitrois is the French reference on this territory.** Founded by Jean-Claude Jitrois, inventor of stretch leather, the house makes its pieces in a Paris workshop, with hand-sewn finishing, and offers made-to-measure including remotely. Leather there is not one material among others but the specialty of the house, which explains the precision reached on the cut.

These two territories do not compete, they answer different expectations. What they share is the starting point of this guide, serious houses name their hides.
